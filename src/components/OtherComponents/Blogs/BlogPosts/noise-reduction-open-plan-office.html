Open-plan offices are built for collaboration, and then fill with people on calls, in stand-ups and at the coffee machine, all within earshot of someone trying to concentrate. The usual complaint is not that the office is loud in general. It is that individual conversations carry across the floor and are hard to ignore. That is a reflection problem, and it can be fixed without putting the walls back up.

Open-office noise at a glance

  • Speech is the main problem, and it travels by bouncing off ceilings, glass and hard floors.
  • The ceiling is usually the biggest untreated surface in an open office, and the first to address.
  • A carpeted floor removes footfall and chair noise across the whole space.
  • Zoning matters as much as materials: keep calls and meetings away from focus desks.
  • Absorb, then separate: screens help most when the ceiling and walls are treated too.

Why are open-plan offices so noisy?

Because most of their surfaces reflect sound. A typical fit-out pairs an exposed or hard ceiling with glass meeting rooms, painted walls and a hard or thin floor finish. A voice that would fade quickly in a furnished room reaches the ceiling, comes back down several desks away, and arrives with enough clarity that people nearby can follow every word. Distracting noise is rarely about volume. It is about intelligibility, and reflected speech stays intelligible a long way across an untreated floor.

Add the knock-on effect: as background noise rises, people speak up to be heard, which raises it again. Breaking that loop is the aim of every fix below.

Seven fixes for open-plan office noise, in order

This ranked list starts with the changes that make the most difference for the least disruption and ends with the structural ones. Most offices need the first four; few need all seven.

  • 1. Absorb sound at the ceiling. Suspended baffles or clouds above desks and collaboration areas catch speech before it travels across the floor. In offices with exposed slabs this is usually the largest single gain.
  • 2. Soften the floor. Carpet tiles take footsteps, chair movement and dropped objects out of the soundscape, everywhere at once.
  • 3. Treat the walls people face. Acoustic panels on the walls behind desks, at the ends of long rooms and opposite glass break up reflections at head height.
  • 4. Zone by activity. Group phone-heavy teams together, put collaboration areas near the kitchen and entrance, and keep focus desks in the quietest corner.
  • 5. Add screens between desks. Acoustic desk screens and free-standing panels block the direct path between neighbours. On their own they do little; combined with ceiling absorption they work well.
  • 6. Treat meeting rooms and phone booths. Glass-walled rooms echo badly inside. Panels on the solid walls and a carpeted floor stop calls sounding hollow and lower the sound level inside, so less carries out when the door opens.
  • 7. Change the layout or build enclosed rooms. If teams that need quiet still sit next to the busiest area, the plan itself needs to change. Enclosed rooms also need their own absorption.

What is the best acoustic solution for an open office?

A combination: absorption overhead, absorption on the walls and a soft floor, arranged around a sensible zoning plan. No single product solves open-plan noise, because speech reaches people by several paths at once. Ceiling treatment deals with the long path over the top of the desks, wall panels deal with reflections at head height, and carpet deals with the floor. Remove one of those paths and you still hear the other two. In large floors, some offices also add a low, even background sound (sound masking) so that distant speech is harder to follow; it works alongside absorption, not instead of it.

The ceiling deserves particular attention because it is the one surface every voice reaches. Baffles hung vertically in rows suit high or exposed ceilings and let services stay visible. Clouds hung flat below the slab suit lower ceilings and zones such as collaboration tables. Both put absorption into the upper volume of the room, which walls cannot reach.

Does carpet reduce noise in an office?

Yes. Carpet absorbs airborne sound that would otherwise reflect off a hard floor, and it greatly reduces impact noise from footsteps, wheeled chairs and trolleys. On a large floor plate that adds up to a noticeably calmer background.

How do you zone an open-plan office for noise?

Arrange the floor from loud to quiet. Place reception, the kitchen, collaboration tables and informal meeting areas together, ideally near the entrance, and put focus desks at the far end, with storage, meeting rooms or phone booths acting as a buffer between the two. Teams that spend the day on calls, such as sales or support, sit best together and away from teams doing concentrated work.

Questions that come up often

What is the cheapest way to reduce noise in an open office?

Rearranging teams so that call-heavy and focus work sit apart costs little and often helps straight away. After that, treating the ceiling usually brings the largest improvement for the area covered.

Do desk screens reduce noise?

Screens block the direct sound between neighbouring desks, but sound still reaches people via the ceiling. They work best combined with ceiling absorption and a carpeted floor.

Can acoustic panels go on a glass wall?

Usually not directly. In glass-walled rooms the simplest approach is to treat the solid walls, the ceiling and the floor, or to use free-standing or frame-mounted panels near the glass.
