Echo in a room is sound reflecting off hard surfaces, worst between parallel ones. To reduce echo in a room, add absorbent surfaces where sound strikes first: acoustic panels on the walls that face each other, a soft floor and, in tall rooms, absorbers hung from the ceiling. Spread them out rather than covering one wall.

Most rooms with an echo problem were never designed to have one. Glass, plaster, stone and polished floors look clean on a drawing and photograph well, but every one of them reflects sound. Put a few people in that room, a television or a video call, and voices start to blur. Conversation gets louder to compensate, which makes the room louder still. The fix is rarely more insulation or thicker walls. It is a small number of soft, absorbent surfaces, placed where the sound actually travels.

How to reduce echo at a glance

  • Treat the walls that face each other first. Parallel hard walls cause the sharpest echo.
  • Soften the floor. Carpet tiles or broadloom take the clatter out of footsteps and chairs.
  • Look up in tall rooms. In double-height spaces and halls, the ceiling becomes a major reflector.
  • Spread treatment around the room. Several panels on different surfaces work better than one large block on one wall.
  • Mount panels off the wall where absorption matters. The same panel absorbs far more with an air gap behind it.

What causes echo in a room?

Echo comes from sound reflecting off hard surfaces and reaching your ears again a fraction of a second after the original sound. In everyday rooms this shows up in two ways. Reverberation is the general wash of sound that hangs in the air after someone stops speaking, the reason a bare hall sounds boomy. Flutter echo is a rapid, ringing repeat between two parallel hard surfaces, such as two plastered walls or a hard floor and a flat ceiling. Clap your hands between two bare walls and you can hear it as a quick metallic buzz.

Both get worse with three things: hard finishes, parallel surfaces and volume. A small bedroom full of soft furnishings rarely echoes. An empty living room with a marble floor, a glass wall and a double-height ceiling almost always does.

How can you tell if a room has an echo problem?

You can check most rooms in a couple of minutes, without instruments. Stand in the middle of the room when it is quiet and work through this list.

  • Clap once, sharply. If the sound rings or buzzes for a moment after your hands stop, there is flutter echo between parallel surfaces. Clap again facing each pair of walls to find which pair.
  • Talk at a normal level. If your voice sounds hollow or louder than you expect, the room is reverberant.
  • Listen on a call. If people on video calls ask you to repeat yourself, or hear the room more than your voice, reflections are reaching the microphone.
  • Count the hard surfaces. Floor, ceiling and at least two walls that are hard and bare usually means the room needs treatment.
  • Notice the noise build-up. If a room gets steadily louder as it fills, people are raising their voices over reflected sound.

If two or more of these sound familiar, the room will benefit from absorption. The checklist also tells you where to start: the pair of surfaces that rang loudest when you clapped.

Where should you treat a room first?

Start with the surfaces sound reaches first and most often. In a typical room that means the walls that face each other at about head height, where voices travel, then the ceiling if the room is tall, then the floor. The diagram shows the order.

The reasoning is simple. Every hard surface a sound wave meets sends most of its energy back into the room. An absorbent surface in that path removes a large part of it, so the next reflection is weaker, and the one after that weaker again. Panels at seated and standing head height catch speech directly. Ceiling absorbers catch the sound that goes up, which in a tall room is a lot of it. A soft floor deals with footsteps, chair legs and the reflection under the table.

Spread the treatment out. Two or three panels on each of the facing walls do more than the same area concentrated on one wall, because they break up the back-and-forth path between them. In meeting rooms, the wall opposite the screen and the wall behind the main seats matter most.

What materials absorb echo?

Soft, porous materials absorb echo: sound that travels into them loses energy among the fibres instead of bouncing straight back. Acoustic panels, carpet, heavy curtains, upholstered furniture and full bookshelves all work this way to some degree. Glass, tile, stone, plaster and lacquered timber barely absorb at all.

The usual measure is NRC, the noise reduction coefficient. It runs from 0 for a surface that reflects everything to about 1 for one that absorbs almost everything, and laboratories measure it to ASTM C423.

Mounting changes the result as much as the material does: the same GAIA PET panel absorbs far more standing off the wall on an air gap than fixed flat against it. Do carpets reduce echo?

Yes. A carpeted floor absorbs sound that would otherwise bounce off tile or stone, and it greatly reduces impact noise from footsteps, heels and chairs. In a room with a hard floor, changing to carpet is often the change people notice first, because footsteps and chair scrapes happen all day.

Carpet cannot do the whole job in a tall or glass-walled room, because sound travelling between the walls and up to the ceiling never touches it. Treat it as one of the three surfaces, not a cure on its own. How do you reduce echo in a large hall or a double-height room?

Treat the ceiling. In a large or tall space, most of the reverberation comes from sound travelling up and back down, and walls alone cannot reach it. Suspended baffles or clouds catch that sound without lowering the whole ceiling. Add panels on the walls at head height and a soft floor where the use allows, and a hall that rang like a station platform becomes usable for speech.

How do you reduce echo in a house?

In a home, start with the room people complain about, usually a living room with a hard floor and a glass wall, or a double-height lounge. A rug or carpeted area under the seating, curtains that can be drawn across glass, and a few acoustic panels on the wall facing the sofa or television make a clear difference. Full bookshelves help too, because the uneven surface scatters sound.

Home offices deserve particular attention because the microphone hears the room. A panel or two on the wall behind your screen and another behind your chair, at head height, will make your voice on calls noticeably clearer. Printed or grooved panels can do this without looking like acoustic treatment, which matters in a room you also live in. How much of a room needs acoustic treatment?

It depends on the room's size, its finishes and what it is used for, so there is no single percentage that suits every space. A small study may need a few panels; a glass-walled boardroom or a banquet hall will need treatment on walls, ceiling and floor. The practical approach is to treat the surfaces the clap test picked out, spread across the room, then judge the result in use. Do acoustic panels soundproof a room?

No. Acoustic panels absorb sound inside a room, which reduces echo and makes speech clearer. Soundproofing stops sound travelling from one room to another, and that depends on the mass and airtightness of the walls, doors, floor and ceiling. If the problem is noise coming through a wall from next door, panels on your side will make your room sound calmer but will not block the neighbour's music.

Which mistakes make echo worse?

Most come from treating too little of the room, or the wrong part of it.

  • Treating one wall only. Sound keeps bouncing between the untreated surfaces. Split the treatment across facing walls.
  • Hanging all the panels high. In rooms used for speech, keep some panels at head height, where they catch the first reflections of voices.
  • Ignoring the ceiling in tall rooms. Walls and floor cannot catch sound that travels upwards first.
  • Closing off the air gap. Packing the cavity behind an air-gap panel with board or solid material, or fixing the panel tight to the wall, gives up the extra absorption the gap was there for.
  • Judging by looks alone. A surface can look acoustic and still absorb little. Ask for tested NRC figures for the product and mounting you plan, not just a sample to look at. Our comparison of acoustic panels and acoustic foam sets out when each makes sense.

Questions that come up often

What absorbs echo in a room?

Soft, porous materials absorb echo: acoustic panels, carpet, heavy curtains and upholstered furniture. Of these, acoustic panels do the most for the wall area they cover, because they are made for the job.

Does furniture reduce echo?

Upholstered sofas, cushions, curtains and full bookshelves all reduce echo a little. They help, but a room with hard walls and a high ceiling usually still needs panels on the walls and absorption overhead.

Why does my new house echo so much?

New homes are often finished in tile, plaster and glass, with little soft furniture yet. Every surface reflects sound, so echo is at its worst before rugs, curtains and furniture arrive.
