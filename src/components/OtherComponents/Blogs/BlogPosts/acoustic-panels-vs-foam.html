Acoustic panels and acoustic foam both absorb sound, but they suit different rooms. PET acoustic panels are rigid, can be cleaned and are made to be seen in finished interiors. Foam suits hidden or studio treatment. Fixed with an air gap, a GAIA PET acoustic panel absorbs 85 to 90 per cent of the sound that reaches it.

The question usually comes up when someone has an echoing room and two very different price lists in front of them. Foam looks like the obvious acoustic product because it is what recording studios are pictured with. PET panels look more like a wall finish. Both work by the same principle, so the real decision is about where the treatment goes, who will see it and how long it has to look good.

Acoustic panels vs foam at a glance

  • Both are porous absorbers: they reduce echo inside a room, and neither soundproofs it.
  • PET panels are rigid boards that can be cut cleanly, mounted with an air gap, printed or grooved, and fixed to walls or hung from ceilings.
  • Foam is soft and light, suited to studios, behind-the-scenes treatment and temporary set-ups.
  • Compare tested NRC values, not thickness or appearance.
  • Ask for fire test documentation for any acoustic product used in a commercial interior.

What is the difference between acoustic panels and acoustic foam?

The material and the way it is used. Acoustic foam is usually an open-cell polyurethane or melamine sponge, cut into wedges, pyramids or flat sheets and glued to a surface. PET acoustic panels are dense boards made from polyester fibres, bonded into a rigid sheet that holds its shape and edges.

Which absorbs more sound, panels or foam?

It depends on the specific product, its thickness and how it is mounted, so compare tested values rather than material types. The measure to ask for is NRC, the noise reduction coefficient, which runs from 0 (reflects everything) to 1 (absorbs everything) and is measured in the laboratory using methods such as ASTM C423.

Mounting matters as much as the material. Fixed flat against a wall, a GAIA PET panel achieves NRC 0.3. Mounted with an air gap behind it, the same panel reaches NRC 0.85–0.90. Because PET panels are rigid, they can stand off a wall on battens or hang from a ceiling as baffles and clouds, which puts the fibres where sound moves air most freely. Foam is almost always glued flat, so it gives up that advantage. Our article on air gaps behind acoustic panels explains why the cavity makes such a difference.

Acoustic panels or foam: which should you use?

Match the product to the room and to how long it needs to last. This side-by-side list covers the decisions that come up most often.

  • Finished interiors people see every day (offices, restaurants, hotels, homes): PET panels. They read as a wall finish, take colour, print and grooves, and keep clean edges.
  • Recording booths, rehearsal rooms and treatment behind fabric: foam is a common choice, because looks matter less and it is easy to cut to odd shapes.
  • Tall rooms and open-plan floors: PET baffles and clouds hung from the ceiling, which foam is not made for.
  • Walls people touch or lean on (corridors, classrooms, receptions): PET panels, because a rigid board holds its shape and can be cleaned.
  • Feature walls and brand walls: printed PET panels, where artwork and absorption share one surface.
  • Short-term or rented spaces where nothing can be screwed to the wall: lightweight treatment, foam or free-standing panels, may be the practical option.

Are acoustic panels worth it?

Where people spend long periods talking, listening or concentrating, yes. A room that echoes makes conversation tiring, calls unclear and restaurants loud, and those problems do not fade with time. A rigid panel that doubles as a wall finish is a one-off installation rather than something to replace every few years. Placed well, a modest number of panels changes how a room feels, which is why the placement advice in our article on how to reduce echo in a room matters as much as the product.

How long do acoustic panels and foam last?

A rigid PET panel holds its shape and edges, and its surface can be cleaned, so it is suited to a fit-out meant to last years. Foam is soft, marks easily, is hard to clean and, being exposed open-cell material, can discolour and crumble at the surface over time, particularly in sunlight. For hidden treatment that matters less. For a wall in a restaurant or a reception, it matters a great deal.

Is acoustic foam a fire risk?

Any acoustic material used in a commercial interior should come with fire test documentation for that specific product, and it should meet the requirements of the building it goes into. That applies equally to foam and to panels. Ask for the test reports before you order, and check them with whoever is responsible for fire safety on the project.

Questions that come up often

Are PET acoustic panels better than foam?

For finished interiors, usually yes: PET panels are rigid, can be cleaned, can be mounted with an air gap and come in colours, grooves and prints. Foam remains a reasonable choice for studios and hidden treatment.

Does acoustic foam soundproof a room?

No. Foam and panels both absorb sound inside a room to reduce echo. Stopping sound passing between rooms depends on the walls, doors, floor and ceiling.

Can acoustic panels be cleaned?

Yes. A rigid PET panel can be cleaned, which is one reason it suits busy commercial spaces. Ask us for the cleaning advice for the finish you choose.
